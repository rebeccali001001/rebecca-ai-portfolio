MODULE 07 · SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

Microservices

Microservices split a system into independently owned and deployable services connected through APIs, events, or queues.

01 · What is it?

What is it?

Microservices is an architectural approach where a product is made of several services, each responsible for a focused business capability and owned by a clear team.

A service has an explicit interface and release boundary. The important idea is not simply “many small programs”; it is that teams can change, deploy, and scale one capability without rebuilding the whole product. That independence also creates network, data, observability, and ownership work.

02 · Think of it like...

Think of it like...

Think of a group of specialist shops that share a street but run their own staff, schedules, and tills.

Each shop can change its own operations, but the customer journey across shops needs coordination. Microservices work similarly: local autonomy trades for more coordination at the boundaries.

03 · What does it do?

What does it do?

Separates ownership

Teams can own a focused capability, its interface, and its operational behavior.

Enables independent release

One service can ship without rebuilding and releasing every other service.

Isolates scaling

A busy capability can receive more instances without scaling all capabilities equally.

Contains failures

A degraded service does not have to make every unrelated capability unavailable.

Makes contracts visible

APIs, events, and queues expose how teams and services depend on one another.

Supports fit-for-purpose design

A service can use the storage or runtime that fits its business responsibility.

04 · Where is it used?

Where is it used?

Microservices can fit large products with multiple teams, distinct scaling needs, or strong reasons to isolate failures. They are not automatically the right choice for a small or early product.

Client→↓API gateway→↓Catalog service→↓Order service
05 · Example

Example

A retailer may run catalog, cart, checkout, payment, fulfillment, and notification as separate services. When a shopper places an order, checkout can call payment synchronously, then publish an order-created event for fulfillment and notifications.

Benefit

Checkout can scale and deploy on its own, while catalog can release search improvements separately.

New cost

A failed network call, duplicate message, or incompatible contract can break a cross-service flow.

Product decision

Use the boundary where team or operational independence is worth its complexity.

PM Perspective: define what the user sees when payment is slow, whether fulfillment can be eventually consistent, and who owns recovery when a dependency fails.

06 · How it connects

How it connects

Services communicate through APIs, messages, or events. A distributed request may cross several processes, so timeouts, retries, duplicate handling, tracing, versioned contracts, and clear ownership become part of the product’s reliability model.

Client→API Gateway→Service APIs→Events / queues

Synchronous API

The caller waits for a result. Useful for an immediate payment decision, but latency and failure remain in the request path.

Asynchronous event

The producer announces that something happened. Useful when downstream work can happen later, but consistency may be delayed.

Message queue

A worker receives buffered work. Useful for smoothing bursts and retries, but messages can be delayed or delivered more than once.

07 · Do Not Confuse

Do Not Confuse

Microservices ≠ many modules

Modules inside one deployable application are not independently deployable microservices.

Microservices ≠ no shared data

Data ownership can be designed in different ways; independent operation is the defining concern.

Microservices ≠ automatically better

They can improve autonomy while increasing operational and coordination costs.

Service ≠ database table

A service owns a capability and behavior; a table is a storage structure.

Independent deploy ≠ independent success

A service can deploy alone but still depend on contracts, identity, queues, data, and other services in a user workflow.

Microservices ≠ serverless

Microservices describes service boundaries; serverless describes an execution and hosting model.

What to Remember
