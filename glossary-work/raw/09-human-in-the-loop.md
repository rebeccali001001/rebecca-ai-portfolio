## Do Not Confuse Candidates

- Human in the Loop vs manual workflow: A manual workflow has a human perform most steps; HITL lets AI perform many steps and adds people at selected control points.
- Human in the Loop vs manual everything: HITL does not mean that a person performs every step.
- Human in the Loop vs no automation: HITL preserves human control while still allowing automation.
- Human in the Loop vs more autonomous flow: HITL requires review for selected actions; a more autonomous flow executes more actions without review. They are points on a spectrum, not always opposites.
- Human in the Loop vs human-on-the-loop: HITL often places a person in a required decision path; human-on-the-loop usually means a person supervises a running system and intervenes when needed.
- Human in the Loop vs human-out-of-the-loop: HITL retains human review or intervention; human-out-of-the-loop removes the person from the operating decision path.
- Human in the Loop vs human-in-command: HITL describes checkpoints inside a workflow; human-in-command emphasizes that a person retains final authority over the system.
- Human review vs human approval: Review means checking or assessing; approval is the explicit authorization to proceed. Review may recommend, reject, or request changes without itself being the authorization.
- Human approval vs automatic validation: Approval is a person’s explicit decision; validation is a system or person checking a rule or condition. A valid action can still require approval.
- Human approval vs automatic prediction: A prediction is a model output; approval is a human authorization decision.
- Human intervention vs human review: Review may be passive checking; intervention changes, stops, or redirects the workflow.
- Human oversight vs human control: Oversight observes and supervises; control requires meaningful authority to change or stop behavior.
- Human oversight vs meaningful human control: Merely watching is not meaningful control if the person lacks information, time, authority, or a real intervention window.
- Human judgment vs model confidence: Human judgment uses context, responsibility, and consequences; confidence is a system score or estimate and is not a substitute for human accountability.
- Approval vs consent: Approval authorizes a workflow action; consent is a person’s permission for a specified use, often involving their rights or data.
- Consent vs authorization: Consent expresses agreement; authorization grants a role or system permission to perform an action. The same person may not be the consent giver and the authorized operator.
- Authorization vs authentication: Authentication establishes who someone is; authorization determines what that identity may do.
- Authorization vs approval: Authorization is an access or permission concept; approval is a decision to allow a particular proposed action.
- Sign-off vs approval: Sign-off is a formal confirmation record; approval is the decision itself. A sign-off may be evidence of approval.
- Validation vs verification: Validation asks whether something meets intended rules or needs; verification asks whether it was built or calculated according to specification. The page only briefly uses validation.
- Validation vs review: Validation may be automatic and rule-based; review may include contextual human judgment.
- Validation vs evaluation: Validation checks a condition or development choice; evaluation measures broader quality or performance.
- Validation vs audit: Validation is a check of a result or condition; an audit examines evidence, process, controls, and compliance over time.
- Review vs evaluation: Review is often a case-level decision; evaluation is a planned measurement of system or output performance.
- Evaluation vs monitoring: Evaluation is a structured measurement; monitoring watches behavior continuously or over time.
- Monitoring vs review: Monitoring detects signals or changes; review is the human examination and decision that may follow.
- Automatic check vs human check: An automatic check applies encoded rules consistently; a human check can interpret context, ambiguity, and consequences.
- Automatic decision vs human decision: An automatic decision follows system logic or model output; a human decision is made by a person who can be accountable for the choice.
- Proposed action vs executed action: A proposal is a suggestion waiting for approval or another condition; an executed action has actually changed a system or external state.
- Recommendation vs action: A recommendation suggests what to do; an action does it. A recommendation may not have side effects.
- Draft vs final output: A draft is subject to review or change; a final output is treated as complete or ready for use.
- Execute vs commit: Execution performs an operation; commit is the point at which a proposed change becomes effective. Some execution can be simulated without committing.
- Act vs observe: Acting changes a state; observing reads or inspects a state or result.
- Observe vs monitor: Observation is a point-in-time input to the agent loop; monitoring is ongoing watching over time.
- Agent vs model: An agent can decide and take actions in a workflow; a model is a component that produces predictions, text, scores, or recommendations.
- Agent vs AI assistant: An assistant may only provide suggestions; an action-taking agent can execute external operations.
- Agent loop vs workflow: An agent loop is a repeated observe-decide-act cycle; a workflow may be a fixed sequence with conditional branches and human steps.
- Agent loop vs human-in-the-loop: The Agent Loop is the system’s recurring cycle; HITL is a control pattern that inserts human review or intervention into that cycle.
- Agent output vs external action: An agent output can be a proposal; an external action changes data, money, permissions, communication, or another system.
- Read-only agent vs write-enabled agent: A read-only agent can retrieve or inspect; a write-enabled agent can create or change external state.
- Advisory mode vs approval mode: Advisory mode recommends without executing; approval mode prepares an action that executes after human authorization.
- Approval mode vs autonomous mode: Approval mode blocks selected actions until a person approves; autonomous mode allows eligible actions to execute without that checkpoint.
- Shadow mode vs production execution: Shadow mode generates recommendations without real side effects; production execution commits changes in the real environment.
- Sandbox vs production environment: A sandbox is isolated for testing; production affects real users, records, money, or systems.
- Dry run vs execution: A dry run simulates or previews effects; execution performs the real action.
- Simulation vs real-world action: Simulation models consequences without necessarily changing reality; a real-world action creates external side effects.
- Human fallback vs human-in-the-loop: A fallback may involve a person only when automation fails; HITL deliberately includes human checkpoints in the normal design.
- Fallback vs escalation: Fallback is a backup path when normal automation cannot safely continue; escalation sends a case to a higher-authority or more specialized reviewer.
- Exception vs error: An exception is a case outside the normal rule and may be valid; an error is an incorrect or failed result or action.
- Exception vs failure: An exception is a routing or condition category; failure means the workflow did not achieve its intended result.
- Ambiguous case vs low-confidence result: Ambiguity concerns the case or interpretation; low confidence concerns how sure the system is. They can occur together but are not identical.
- Low confidence vs incorrect result: Low confidence may be correct but uncertain; an incorrect result is wrong regardless of confidence.
- Risk vs uncertainty: Risk combines possible outcomes with their likelihood and impact; uncertainty is lack of clarity or knowledge and may not have a quantified impact.
- Risk threshold vs confidence threshold: A risk threshold concerns potential harm or impact; a confidence threshold concerns how sure the system is.
- High-impact action vs high-risk action: Impact describes consequence magnitude; risk also considers likelihood. A high-impact action can be low risk if tightly controlled, and a lower-impact action can be high risk if frequent or likely to fail.
- Reversible vs irreversible action: A reversible action can be undone; an irreversible action cannot easily be restored, so it generally deserves stronger controls.
- Pre-action approval vs post-action review: Pre-action approval can prevent the side effect; post-action review can detect or correct problems after execution.
- Preventive control vs detective control: Preventive controls stop an error before it occurs; detective controls find it after or as it occurs.
- Corrective control vs compensating control: Corrective controls fix a problem or effect; compensating controls provide an alternative safeguard when the preferred control is unavailable.
- Fail-safe vs fail-closed: Fail-safe means moving to a safer state; fail-closed specifically blocks access or action by default. They overlap in some designs but are not exact synonyms.
- Fail-closed vs fail-open: Fail-closed denies or blocks on failure; fail-open allows or proceeds on failure. The choice depends on safety, availability, and context.
- Safe default vs default choice: A safe default is chosen to limit harm; a default choice is merely the preselected option and may not be safe.
- Graceful degradation vs failure: Graceful degradation continues with reduced capability and safety; failure means the intended workflow does not complete.
- Shutdown vs rollback: Shutdown stops operation; rollback restores an earlier state. A system may need both.
- Rollback vs cancellation: Rollback reverses an already applied change; cancellation stops a pending action before it is applied.
- Cancel vs reject: Cancel stops a pending request or action; reject is a decision not to authorize a proposal. A rejected action may be recorded as a decision.
- Modify vs override: Modify changes the proposal; override replaces or overrules an automated decision or behavior.
- Override vs bypass: Override is an authorized replacement decision; bypass circumvents a control. An override can be legitimate; a bypass may be unauthorized.
- Emergency override vs ordinary override: Emergency override is exceptional authority used under urgent conditions; an ordinary override is a normal controlled correction.
- Policy vs rule: A policy is a broader governing statement; a rule is a concrete condition or instruction derived from or implementing it.
- Policy vs constraint: A policy governs acceptable behavior; a constraint limits available actions. A policy may contain multiple constraints.
- Policy violation vs exception: A violation is disallowed behavior; an exception is a formally recognized case that may have a different approved path.
- Exception approval vs ordinary approval: Exception approval authorizes departure from a normal rule; ordinary approval authorizes an action within the expected policy path.
- Approval threshold vs spending limit: An approval threshold triggers human review; a spending limit caps what may be spent. An action can be under the threshold yet still exceed a hard limit.
- Approval threshold vs transaction limit: A threshold controls routing to approval; a limit controls whether the transaction is allowed at all.
- Risk appetite vs risk threshold: Risk appetite is the organization’s accepted level of risk; a risk threshold is an operational cutoff for routing or control.
- Risk acceptance vs approval: Risk acceptance formally accepts residual risk; approval authorizes a proposed action. Approving an action does not always mean accepting all residual risk.
- Risk owner vs control owner: The risk owner manages the risk; the control owner operates a specific control that addresses it.
- Process owner vs system owner: The process owner owns the business workflow; the system owner owns the technical system. One person may hold both roles, but the responsibilities differ.
- Accountable vs responsible: Responsible means performing the work; accountable means answerable for the final outcome.
- Reviewer vs approver: A reviewer examines a case; an approver has authority to authorize or reject it. The same person can have both roles only if governance allows it.
- Operator vs reviewer: An operator runs or supervises the system; a reviewer checks a case or result. An operator may not be independent enough to approve their own action.
- Subject-matter expert vs approver: An expert contributes knowledge; an approver has formal decision authority. One person can be both.
- Separation of duties vs four-eyes principle: Separation of duties divides responsibilities; four-eyes requires a second person to check an important action. Four-eyes is one way to implement separation.
- Independent review vs second opinion: Independent review requires separation from conflicting incentives; a second opinion is simply another judgment and may not be fully independent.
- Consensus vs majority approval: Consensus seeks broad agreement; majority approval requires more than half, even if a minority disagrees.
- Veto vs rejection: A veto is a special authority to block despite other support; rejection is an ordinary decision not to approve.
- Appeal vs reconsideration: An appeal formally challenges a decision; reconsideration is the act of reviewing it again, which may be requested without a formal appeal.
- Due process vs fairness: Due process concerns fair, reviewable procedure; fairness can also concern whether the outcome treats people appropriately.
- Procedural fairness vs substantive fairness: Procedural fairness concerns how the decision was made; substantive fairness concerns the result or treatment itself.
- Contestability vs explainability: Contestability is the ability to challenge a decision; explainability is the ability to understand why it was made.
- Notice vs consent: Notice informs a person; consent asks for permission. Being notified does not mean a person agreed.
- Informed consent vs forced consent: Informed consent is understood and freely given; forced consent lacks a meaningful alternative or choice.
- Human agency vs human oversight: Agency is a person’s ability to choose and act; oversight is observation and supervision of a system.
- Meaningful choice vs default choice: Meaningful choice is informed and can change the outcome; default choice is what happens when no change is made.
- Human factors vs usability: Human factors cover human abilities, limitations, and behavior; usability focuses on how easy and effective an interface or workflow is to use.
- Alert fatigue vs review fatigue: Alert fatigue comes from too many alerts; review fatigue comes from repetitive review work. Both can degrade judgment.
- Automation bias vs confirmation bias: Automation bias favors system output because it is automated; confirmation bias favors information supporting an existing belief.
- Anchoring vs overreliance: Anchoring fixates on an initial value or proposal; overreliance broadly trusts the system more than justified.
- Rubber-stamp approval vs meaningful approval: A rubber stamp is nominal and uncritical; meaningful approval is informed, authorized, and able to change the outcome.
- Nominal oversight vs meaningful oversight: Nominal oversight exists on paper; meaningful oversight has enough information, time, authority, and intervention power.
- Box-checking vs compliance: Box-checking completes formal steps without substance; genuine compliance follows the purpose and requirements of the control.
- Compliance theater vs responsible governance: Compliance theater creates an appearance of safety; responsible governance actually manages risk and accountability.
- Auditability vs transparency: Auditability means reconstructing events after the fact; transparency means making behavior and decisions visible to relevant people.
- Traceability vs explainability: Traceability follows what happened and where it came from; explainability gives reasons for behavior or decisions.
- Provenance vs attribution: Provenance describes source and history; attribution links an action or output to its actor or owner.
- Audit log vs decision log: An audit log records system and user events; a decision log focuses on decisions and their reasons.
- Event log vs audit trail: An event log is a time-ordered technical record; an audit trail is a broader evidence trail for accountability and review.
- Immutable log vs ordinary log: An immutable log is designed to resist changes; an ordinary log may be editable or less strongly protected.
- Evidence vs rationale: Evidence supports a decision; rationale explains how the evidence led to the decision.
- Context vs evidence: Context is surrounding information; evidence is information specifically supporting or challenging a claim or action.
- Information sufficiency vs information overload: Sufficiency means enough information to decide; overload means so much information that decision quality suffers.
- Review packet vs context window: A review packet is the organized material shown to a human; context window can refer to the total information available for the decision and may be used technically for an AI as well.
- Accuracy vs correctness: Accuracy is a measured rate or degree of being right; correctness is whether a particular result or action is right.
- Quality vs safety: Quality is broader usefulness and correctness; safety focuses on avoiding unacceptable harm.
- Reliability vs robustness: Reliability is consistent dependability; robustness is maintaining acceptable behavior under variation or disturbance.
- Suitability vs usefulness: Suitability is fit for a context or intended use; usefulness is how much it helps accomplish a goal.
- Review accuracy vs reviewer agreement: Review accuracy compares decisions with the correct outcome; reviewer agreement compares reviewers with each other. Reviewers can agree and still be wrong.
- Precision vs recall: Precision asks how many flagged cases are truly relevant; recall asks how many relevant cases were found.
- False positive vs false negative: A false positive flags a case that is not positive or risky; a false negative misses a case that is positive or risky.
- Review coverage vs review rate: Coverage asks whether relevant/risky cases are reviewed; review rate is the overall share routed to review. High review rate does not guarantee high coverage.
- Approval rate vs approval quality: Approval rate is how often proposals are approved; quality asks whether those approvals were correct and appropriate.
- Automation rate vs autonomy: Automation rate measures work done without direct human performance; autonomy concerns independent decision and action authority.
- Throughput vs latency: Throughput is work completed per period; latency is time for one request or action.
- Review latency vs approval turnaround time: Review latency can include waiting and checking; approval turnaround time specifically measures request-to-decision time.
- SLA vs SLO: An SLA is a service commitment, often with obligations; an SLO is an internal or external target. They are related but not identical.
- KPI vs metric: A metric is any measurable value; a KPI is a metric selected as especially important for performance management.
- MTTI vs MTTR: MTTI measures time until a person intervenes; MTTR measures time until an incident or issue is resolved or repaired.
- Queue vs backlog: A queue is ordered waiting work; a backlog is accumulated unfinished work and may not have strict processing order.
- Triage vs routing: Triage prioritizes or categorizes cases; routing sends them to a path or responsible person.
- Routing vs assignment: Routing selects a workflow path; assignment selects a particular reviewer or owner.
- Skill-based routing vs round-robin assignment: Skill-based routing considers expertise; round-robin distributes sequentially regardless of specialization.
- Queue fairness vs priority: Priority intentionally orders urgent or risky work; queue fairness prevents lower-priority work from being neglected indefinitely.
- Bottleneck vs throughput constraint: A bottleneck is the constrained process component; a throughput constraint is the resulting limit on work per time.
- Human availability vs reviewer capacity: Availability means someone can respond; capacity includes how much work they can handle without overload.
- Escalation path vs approval path: An approval path is the normal authorization route; an escalation path is used when a case needs higher authority or expertise.
- Approval queue vs review queue: An approval queue contains actions awaiting authorization; a review queue can contain cases needing assessment even if no authorization is required.
- Asynchronous review vs synchronous review: Asynchronous review can happen later while other work proceeds; synchronous review blocks the workflow while a person decides.
- Blocking approval vs non-blocking review: Blocking approval prevents execution until approval; non-blocking review may observe or assess without stopping immediate execution.
- Pre-action approval vs approval after execution: Pre-action approval can prevent the action; approval after execution cannot be the same preventive gate.
- 100% review vs selective review: 100% review checks every case; selective review checks only chosen cases or samples.
- Sampled review vs periodic review: Sampled review selects cases; periodic review selects times. They can be combined.
- Continuous monitoring vs periodic review: Monitoring watches continuously; periodic review happens at scheduled intervals.
- Event-driven review vs periodic review: Event-driven review starts after a trigger; periodic review starts because the calendar says so.
- Post-action review vs incident response: Post-action review can be routine; incident response handles a notable failure, harm, or security event.
- Incident response vs remediation: Incident response coordinates immediate handling; remediation fixes underlying problems and restores safer operation.
- Root cause analysis vs blame assignment: Root cause analysis seeks system and process causes; blame assignment seeks a person to fault and may obscure systemic causes.
- Corrective action vs preventive action: Corrective action fixes an existing problem; preventive action reduces the chance of recurrence or a future problem.
- CAPA vs incident response: CAPA is a structured corrective/preventive improvement process; incident response is immediate operational handling.
- Monitoring vs observability: Monitoring tracks known signals; observability is the broader ability to understand internal state from outputs, logs, traces, and metrics.
- Drift vs stale data: Drift is a change in distributions or relationships over time; stale data is data no longer current for a particular decision.
- Stale approval vs approval expiry: Stale approval may be invalid because context changed even before formal expiry; expiry is a defined time boundary.
- Approval replay vs duplicate approval: Replay reuses an old approval for a new request; duplicate approval repeats approval for the same request.
- Idempotency vs rollback: Idempotency makes repetition safe; rollback reverses a change after it happened.
- Transaction integrity vs auditability: Transaction integrity ensures the change is correct and complete; auditability ensures the history can be inspected.
- Tool permission vs tool approval: Tool permission grants an agent the ability to call a tool; tool approval authorizes a particular call or action.
- Tool call vs external side effect: A tool call is a request to a capability; it may be read-only or may produce an external side effect.
- API action vs tool call: An API action is an operation through an interface; a tool call is an agent-level request that may use an API underneath.
- Side effect vs output: An output is returned information; a side effect changes external state.
- Spending limit vs side-effect budget: A spending limit concerns money; a side-effect budget can limit any external change or action count.
- Blast radius vs impact: Blast radius is the scope of possible change or harm; impact is the magnitude or consequence of an outcome.
- Read-only vs non-destructive: Read-only means no writes by design; non-destructive may still write reversible or low-impact changes.
- Write-enabled vs autonomous: Write-enabled means the agent has permission to change state; autonomous means it may decide and act without human intervention. One does not guarantee the other.
- Constrained agent vs human-in-the-loop agent: A constrained agent is limited by rules or permissions; a HITL agent also routes selected decisions through a person.
- Policy-as-code vs policy engine: Policy-as-code is the representation of policy as executable logic; the policy engine is the component that evaluates it.
- Rules engine vs model: A rules engine applies explicit rules; a model learns statistical patterns and produces outputs. A workflow can use both.
- Decision engine vs human decision: A decision engine selects using programmed logic; a human decision uses a person’s judgment and responsibility.
- Workflow engine vs workflow: A workflow is the process definition; the workflow engine executes and coordinates it.
- State machine vs workflow: A state machine emphasizes states and transitions; a workflow emphasizes tasks and sequence. A workflow may be implemented as a state machine.
- Precondition vs approval: A precondition is a requirement that must be true; approval is a person’s authorization. Approval may be one precondition.
- Postcondition vs outcome: A postcondition is an expected condition after an action; an outcome is what actually happened, which may or may not satisfy it.
- Invariant vs constraint: An invariant must remain true throughout execution; a constraint limits possible choices and may apply only at a particular step.
- Scope of authority vs scope of review: Authority defines what a role may approve or do; review scope defines what the person is expected to inspect.
- Delegated authority vs retained accountability: Delegated authority can move decision power; accountability may remain with the delegating person or organization.
- Shared responsibility vs accountability gap: Shared responsibility divides clear duties; an accountability gap means no one clearly owns the outcome.
- Governance gap vs control gap: A governance gap is missing rules or roles; a control gap is a missing or ineffective safeguard for a specific risk.
- Human error vs automation error: Human error comes from people; automation error comes from system logic, data, or model behavior. Both need controls.
- Omission vs commission: Omission is failing to do a needed step; commission is doing a prohibited or incorrect step.
- Coordination error vs individual error: Coordination error arises at handoffs or interfaces; individual error arises from one actor’s action or omission.
- Automation failure vs HITL failure: Automation failure is a system component failure; HITL failure is a poorly designed or ineffective human control.
- Human review failure vs model failure: Review failure is a human-control problem; model failure is a prediction or generation problem. They can compound each other.
- Workflow deadlock vs queue backlog: Deadlock prevents progress through mutual waiting; backlog is accumulated work that may still progress.
- Orphaned task vs abandoned review: An orphaned task has no owner; an abandoned review has an owner or start but was not completed.
- SLA miss vs timeout: An SLA miss violates a service target; a timeout is a system or workflow limit that may trigger a fallback.
- Retry vs re-review: Retry repeats an action or attempt; re-review asks a person to examine a case again, often after new information or changes.
- Re-review vs reconsideration: Re-review repeats checking; reconsideration reopens a decision, often with new evidence or an appeal.
- Correction path vs rollback: A correction path changes the next action; rollback restores an earlier state after a change.
- Manual takeover vs fallback-to-manual: Manual takeover is the act of a person assuming control; fallback-to-manual is the designed path that makes it possible.
- Failover vs fallback: Failover switches to another service or process; fallback is any backup path, which may be manual.
- Business continuity vs disaster recovery: Continuity keeps essential operations going during disruption; disaster recovery restores systems after a major failure.
- Operational resilience vs robustness: Resilience covers enduring and recovering from disruption; robustness covers maintaining acceptable behavior under variation.
- Human readiness vs reviewer competence: Readiness includes availability, access, training, and process support; competence is the ability to perform the role well.
- Situational awareness vs context: Situational awareness is the human’s understanding of current state and risk; context is the information available to support it.
- Vigilance vs attention allocation: Vigilance is sustained alertness; attention allocation decides where limited attention should be spent.
- Human attention budget vs staffing: Attention budget is cognitive capacity; staffing is the number and availability of people.
- Review interface vs approval workflow: The interface is what the reviewer uses; the workflow is the underlying sequence, rules, and state transitions.
- Action preview vs simulation: A preview shows intended effects; a simulation estimates behavior or outcome in a modelled environment.
- Diff vs impact preview: A diff shows exact data/content changes; an impact preview shows likely consequences, which may be broader.
- Rationale vs evidence: Rationale is the explanation for a decision; evidence is the supporting information.
- Explanation vs justification: An explanation describes why a system behaved; a justification argues why the behavior should be accepted. A good review may need both.
- Interpretability vs explainability: Interpretability concerns how understandable the internal behavior is; explainability concerns communicating reasons to people. The page does not define either in detail.
- Human evaluation vs human approval: Evaluation judges quality; approval authorizes an action.
- User acceptance testing vs production approval: UAT checks whether users find the system fit for use; production approval authorizes live deployment.
- Red teaming vs ordinary testing: Red teaming actively searches for adversarial weaknesses; ordinary testing may check expected behavior.
- Scenario test vs edge-case test: Scenario testing covers representative situations; edge-case testing targets unusual boundary conditions.
- Stress test vs load test: Stress testing pushes beyond normal conditions to find failure behavior; load testing measures performance under expected or specified load.
- Design review vs operational review: Design review checks the proposed architecture; operational review checks running workflow performance and controls.
- Threat modeling vs hazard analysis: Threat modeling focuses on malicious threats and security; hazard analysis focuses on conditions that can cause harm, including non-malicious failures.
- Risk register vs control register: A risk register tracks risks; a control register tracks safeguards and owners.
- Evidence package vs review packet: An evidence package supports an audit or assurance claim; a review packet supports one reviewer’s case decision.
- Assurance case vs safety case: An assurance case can support broader claims such as security or reliability; a safety case focuses on safety.
- High-risk AI vs high-impact action: High-risk AI describes the use or system risk; high-impact action describes a particular action’s consequences.
- Regulated use case vs high-stakes decision: A regulated use case is governed by law or regulation; a high-stakes decision has serious consequences even if not explicitly regulated.
- Due process vs human-in-the-loop: Adding a human checkpoint does not automatically provide fair procedure, notice, evidence, or appeal.
- Human rights impact vs customer impact: Human-rights impact concerns rights and freedoms; customer impact is broader effect on a customer and may be commercial or operational.
- Responsible AI vs human-in-the-loop: HITL is one control pattern; responsible AI is a broader set of safety, fairness, privacy, transparency, and accountability practices.
- Trustworthy AI vs trust: Trustworthy AI is a system property or design goal; trust is a person’s or organization’s belief in it.
- Human-centered AI vs human-in-the-loop: Human-centered AI covers design around human goals and agency; HITL is a specific workflow control pattern.
- Augmentation vs replacement: Augmentation supports human capability; replacement removes a human role. HITL commonly supports augmentation but can coexist with partial replacement.
- Decision support vs decision automation: Decision support helps a person decide; decision automation delegates the decision to the system.
- Copilot vs autopilot: A copilot assists while a person remains in control; an autopilot performs more actions independently.
- Recommendation system vs action-taking agent: A recommendation system suggests options; an action-taking agent can execute external changes.
- Customer refund vs financial action: A customer refund is one financial use case; financial action also includes payments and other money operations.
- Refund policy vs policy limit: A refund policy is the whole rule set; the policy limit is one amount or boundary inside it.
- Read order vs retrieve data: Reading an order is the page’s concrete example; retrieving data is the broader capability.
- Calculate refund vs approve refund: Calculation determines an amount; approval authorizes sending it.
- Finance manager vs approver: A finance manager is a role/person in the example; approver is the functional authority for a particular action.
- Private system vs sensitive access: A private system is the protected resource; sensitive access is permission to use or view it.
- Personal data vs sensitive data: Personal data identifies or relates to a person; sensitive data is a broader risk category and may include non-personal secrets.
- External communication vs publication: External communication sends information outside; publication makes it available to an audience, often more broadly.
- Delete vs overwrite: Delete removes data; overwrite replaces existing data. Both can be destructive and deserve review.
- Send email vs publish: Sending email targets recipients; publishing makes content available through a public or shared channel.
- Agent continues vs workflow completes: The agent continuing means it resumes another step; the overall workflow may still have later actions or checks.
- Human review is a control gate vs approval for every step: A control gate is inserted where judgment, authorization, or risk matters; HITL does not require approval of every operation.
- Human approval vs human does everything: Approval is one human decision point; it does not mean the human performs the preparation, calculation, or execution steps.
- Validation vs human approval in the refund example: Checking refund amount against a policy limit is validation; a finance manager approving the refund is human approval.
- HITL vs autonomous flow as a binary: The page explicitly presents a spectrum; systems can mix automatic execution, selective review, and different thresholds.
- Human review vs every AI request: HITL uses selected review points, not necessarily review of every request.
- Human in the loop vs no automation: The human controls selected risk boundaries while AI may still read, calculate, prepare, and recommend.
